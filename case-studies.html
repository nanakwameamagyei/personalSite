Data Curation as Data Governance in a European Research Infrastructure

Relevant for research infrastructures, public-sector data initiatives, sustainability programmes, and organisations working with Open Data or FAIR principles.

1. Context

This case is situated within the European Long-Term Ecosystem, Critical Zone, and Socio-ecological Research (eLTER) network; a distributed environmental research infrastructure comprising over 600 research sites across 26 European countries. The network is coordinated under the European Strategy Forum on Research Infrastructures (ESFRI).

The research stations collect long-term environmental data (biodiversity, climate, ecological indicators) to support scientific research and sustainability policy. The case matters because it exposes the sociotechnical realities of implementing Internet-of-Things sensors, Open Data and FAIR principles at scale in long-lived, heterogeneous infrastructures.

2. The Core Problem

While ESFRI and funding bodies mandated open data sharing, the collaborative work required to make data reusable was largely invisible and undervalued. Data curation activities such as cleaning, documenting, contextualising, and maintaining datasets, were fragmented across sites and unevenly resourced.

  • Coordination challenges across disciplines, technologies, and countries
  • Semantic and technical heterogeneity in data formats and metadata
  • Collective action problems and weak incentives for data sharing
  • Governance gaps between policy mandates and everyday practice

3. My Role

I was a PhD researcher embedded within the infrastructure over four years (2020–2024). I conducted a longitudinal qualitative case study across four research stations in Norway and Portugal.

My role combined ethnographic research, interpretive analysis, and knowledge synthesis. I translated empirical findings into academic contributions and practical insights for data governance.

4. Approach

My approach was interpretive and practice-oriented, grounded in sociotechnical systems thinking.

  • System mapping of governance structures, data flows, and institutional arrangements
  • Stakeholder engagement with data curators, scientists, technicians, managers, and policymakers
  • Semi-structured interviews (37)
  • Field observations (~96 hours)
  • Document analysis of policies, guidelines, and reports

Analytically, I combined concepts from information infrastructures, sensemaking, and polycentric governance to study data curation as a form of governance enacted through practice.

5. Key Decisions & Trade-offs

  • Prioritised everyday practice over formal governance frameworks
  • Embraced organisational and technical messiness instead of idealised automation
  • Focused on depth and contextual insight rather than broad generalisation
  • Combined theories across IS, CSCW, and STS to address sociotechnical complexity

6. Outcomes

Theoretical contributions:

  • A framework conceptualising collaborative data curation as data governance
  • Six peer-reviewed publications across Information Systems and CSCW venues

Practical insights:

  • Highlighted the central role of metadata, provenance, and local expertise
  • Identified training gaps and under-resourced curation roles
  • Demonstrated the limits of top-down governance in heterogeneous infrastructures

7. What This Demonstrates

This case shows that effective data governance is enacted through everyday work, not merely through policy or technology. It demonstrates the value of polycentric, flexible governance models and the importance of recognising human judgement and local autonomy in long-term data infrastructures.

Designing a Conversational Skills Training System for Autistic Adolescents

Relevant for education technology, digital health, inclusive design, and human-centred AI systems.

1. Context

This case is based on my MSc ICT dissertation, which focused on the design and implementation of a digital conversational skills training system for adolescents diagnosed with Asperger’s Syndrome or High-Functioning Autism.

Many adolescents on the autism spectrum struggle with conversation initiation, turn-taking, and response timing, which affects social participation, learning outcomes, and self-confidence. Existing interventions were largely therapist-led, expensive, and difficult to scale.

2. The Core Problem

The core challenge was to design a technology-supported intervention that could help adolescents practice conversational skills in a safe, repeatable, and engaging way — without reducing social interaction to rigid scripts or removing necessary human judgement.

  • Capturing the timing and flow of natural conversation
  • Balancing structure with flexibility
  • Ensuring ethical sensitivity and user dignity
  • Designing for non-technical users

3. My Role

I acted as the sole researcher and system designer, responsible for problem framing, requirements analysis, system design, implementation, and evaluation.

4. Approach

  • User- and literature-driven requirements analysis
  • Design science methodology with iterative prototyping
  • Focus on conversation initiation, response timing, and feedback loops
  • Ethical considerations embedded directly into system design

5. Key Decisions & Trade-offs

  • Guided practice instead of full automation
  • Learning support over performance scoring
  • Usability and dignity over behavioural realism
  • Adaptability over one-size-fits-all interaction models

6. Outcomes

  • A functional prototype for conversational skills training
  • A validated sociotechnical design framework
  • Recognition as an exemplary MSc dissertation

7. What This Demonstrates

This case demonstrates my ability to translate complex human and social needs into ethical, usable digital systems — combining research, system design, and implementation while respecting human judgement and dignity.

Implementing Digital Workplaces for Public-Sector Transformation in Ghana

Public-sector digitalisation, information governance, and organisational change.

1. Context

As a junior technical consultant at eSolutions Consulting and STL Group Ghana, I was part of multidisciplinary teams supporting digital transformation initiatives across Ghanaian public-sector organisations.

Clients included the Ministry of Finance, Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), Ghana Gas, and the National Development Planning Commission (NDPC). These institutions were under pressure to modernise operations, improve information security, and reduce reliance on paper-based workflows.

2. The Core Problem

Many government agencies relied on fragmented, paper-heavy processes and on-premise systems that limited collaboration, transparency, and efficiency. Remote work was difficult, document security was inconsistent, and information visibility across units was poor.

The challenge was not only technical, but organisational:

  • Low digital literacy among staff
  • Concerns about data security and access control
  • Resistance to changing established work practices
  • Need for systems that aligned with public-sector governance structures

3. My Role

I worked as a technical consultant within the delivery teams, involved across the full lifecycle of implementation.

  • Configuring and customising SharePoint environments
  • Supporting system design and deployment
  • Training public-sector staff and administrators
  • Providing hands-on support during adoption and rollout

4. Approach

At eSolutions Consulting, we developed the Smart Workplace platform, initially as an on-premise solution and later migrated to Microsoft 365.

The platform was designed to eliminate the need for a physical, paper-based office by enabling secure document management, collaboration, and remote access.

  • Secure, role-based access to organisational documents
  • Remote collaboration and information sharing
  • Improved visibility across departments
  • Reduced operational and administrative costs

5. Key Decisions & Trade-offs

  • Balanced security controls with ease of use
  • Prioritised staff training over purely technical optimisation
  • Accepted gradual adoption rather than forced organisational change
  • Transitioned from on-premise systems to cloud-based infrastructure

6. Outcomes

  • Successful deployment of Smart Workplace across multiple public institutions
  • Improved document security and information accessibility
  • Increased productivity and support for remote work
  • Adoption by government agencies, including the National Information Technology Agency (NITA)

7. What This Demonstrates

This case demonstrates my ability to deliver technology in complex public-sector environments where governance, human capacity, and institutional constraints matter as much as technical capability. It shows practical experience in digital transformation, information governance, and organisational change in real-world settings.
